


Hi, could you please explain briefly what are the implications of having multiple individuals per species in the phylogeny, e.g, when you have some widespread species ocurring in different areas and you want to include one individual per area?

A: 

* DEC, DEC+J, etc. assume that it is reasonable for species to occupy 1 or more areas

(the d = range expansion, e=range loss processes assume this)

* So, *if* your clade has species that all only occupy a single area, then using a tree of specimens, with 1 specimen per species, is fine

* But, if your clade has some species that occupy multiple ranges, a tree of specimens will be very misleading, because each specimen lives in a single area.

Single-area ranges strongly favour +J model variants.
(fine if truly 1 area, but not otherwise)

Solution: simplify the tree to 1 specimen per species

Trees can pruned with e.g.:

ape::drop.tip

Q: I still have a question about whether the analysis should include outgroups. If I'm focusing on the original place of a family, i.e, the reconstructed results of the root nodes, should the Newick tree I use include outgroups of that family?

A: Generally, yes include outgroups if possible.  Typically, the best answers for estimating the state/range at node F (the root node of a Family) would include multiple sister groups of clade F.  This way, node F is in the middle of the tree, not the bottom.  It gets information from both the clade F species, and the sister groups.

Also: bigger trees just contain more data, thus more information to estimate the processes operating. Presumably, more accurate inference of rates/processes leads to more accurate ancestral range estimates. 

(The big assumption is homogeneity of the process, ie it's working about the same across the tree. That could be wrong, but then that's true of almost anything in macroevolution.)


Q: Does the distribution range of the tips within the family or the distribution range of outgroup have a greater impact on the root node? 

A: generally, the tips within the family have a greater impact. Typically, ranges are somewhat conserved, so typically much of what is in the tips was inherited from their common ancestor. 

Big counterexample: situations like marsupisals - South American -> Antarctica -> Australia

If you were doing an Australia clade analysis, it would be hard to infer Antarctica origins without e.g. fossil outgroups, and a connectivity matrix allowing that area to be occupied in the past, if not now. 

Trees of living species can be biased, if extinction was biased e.g. geographically
